Research

My research explores how cancer cells adapt to the tissue environments they encounter during metastasis. I am particularly interested in how environmental stresses, including low oxygen and altered nutrient availability, reshape cellular metabolism and gene regulation. My postdoctoral work addresses these questions through two connected projects that combine functional genomics, metabolic profiling, protein tracking, and experimental models of cancer.

Unbiased CRISPR Screening to Discover Metabolic Dependencies of Bone Colonization

I investigate the metabolic programs that allow prostate cancer cells to survive and grow within bone. A central goal is to identify genes and pathways that become especially important in the bone microenvironment, providing insight into how cancer cells establish metastatic colonies.

To address this question, I use unbiased pooled CRISPR screening of metabolic genes in the Bone-in-Culture Array (BICA), an ex vivo platform that supports tumor-cell growth within bone tissue. By comparing the effects of gene disruption in BICA with those in conventional cell culture, I can distinguish dependencies associated with the bone environment from those required for general cell survival and proliferation.

These screens provide a foundation for investigating how lipid metabolism, redox balance, calcium signaling, and other metabolic processes contribute to bone colonization. Follow-up studies use targeted genetic perturbations, metabolic profiling, and complementary prostate cancer models to determine how candidate pathways support adaptation. The broader aim is to connect genetic dependencies with biological mechanisms and identify metabolic vulnerabilities that could inform future strategies to prevent or treat bone metastasis.

Stress-Dependent Mitochondrial-to-Nuclear Trafficking and Chromatin Accessibility

My second project investigates whether environmental stress changes the location and function of mitochondrial metabolic enzymes, focusing on IDH2 and ACO2. I am interested in whether these enzymes enter the nucleus under conditions such as hypoxia, and whether their nuclear activity influences chromatin accessibility and gene expression.

A central question is the origin of the nuclear enzyme pool. Nuclear proteins could arise from newly synthesized enzymes that bypass mitochondrial import, or from enzymes that first enter mitochondria and subsequently relocate to the nucleus. Distinguishing these routes is essential for understanding how mitochondrial metabolism communicates with nuclear gene regulation.

I am developing approaches that combine protein tagging, compartment-specific tracking, controlled protein release, high-resolution imaging, and biochemical fractionation to investigate these possibilities. These approaches aim to establish where the enzymes originate, when they accumulate in the nucleus, and how their localization changes in response to stress.

I also aim to determine whether nuclear IDH2 and ACO2 alter local metabolite availability, histone modifications, and chromatin accessibility. By linking protein movement to metabolic activity and gene regulation, this project seeks to explain how communication between mitochondria and the nucleus helps cancer cells adapt to stressful environments during metastatic progression.

Mitochondrial ROS & Skeletal Aging (PhD Work)

Showed that mitochondrial oxidative stress in osteoblast-lineage cells disrupts PTH signaling, iron metabolism, and proteostasis through single-cell RNA-seq analysis — but does not drive cellular senescence as previously assumed. This supports multi-target combination approaches for age-associated osteoporosis.

Chemotherapy-Induced Bone Loss (PhD Work)

Demonstrated that standard breast-cancer chemotherapy (doxorubicin + cyclophosphamide + docetaxel) causes persistent bone loss up to 12 months after treatment cessation. Importantly, senolytic drugs did not prevent this damage, challenging the assumption that therapy-induced senescent cells are the primary driver.
